Solar Wind

[SOH-ler wind] · the Sun's outward breath of plasma

The solar wind is a continuous flow of charged particles — mostly protons and electrons — boiling off the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona. The corona is so hot, over a million degrees, that the Sun's gravity cannot hold it; the gas simply streams away in every direction, filling the whole solar system with a thin, electrically charged breeze.

At Earth's distance the wind is faint — only a handful of particles per cubic centimetre — yet it never stops, and it carries the Sun's magnetic field with it. When a storm on the Sun flings out a denser, faster gust, that gust can shake Earth's own magnetic field hard enough to light the aurora, disturb compasses, and ripple through power grids.

How it was discovered

For centuries comet tails offered a clue: they always point away from the Sun, no matter which way the comet travels, as if blown by some invisible current. In the 1950s the physicist Eugene Parker argued the corona must be expanding outward supersonically. The idea was doubted until spacecraft flew straight through the stream and measured it directly, confirming a wind that is always on.

Why it matters

The solar wind blows a vast bubble, the heliosphere, that shields the planets from much of the harsher radiation drifting between the stars. Nearer home it governs “space weather” — the swings in the near-Earth environment that engineers now forecast to protect satellites, radio links, and astronauts. The same gusts that endanger a spacecraft are the ones that paint the polar sky.
