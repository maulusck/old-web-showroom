Aurora Borealis

[aw-ROR-uh bor-ee-AL-is] · also “the Northern Lights”

The aurora borealis is a luminous display in the night sky of high northern latitudes, produced when charged particles streaming from the Sun collide with gases in the upper atmosphere. Each collision nudges an atom into an excited state; as the atom settles, it releases a single quiet packet of light. A whole sky of them becomes a curtain that ripples, folds, and fades over minutes.

The colours are a fingerprint of altitude and gas. Oxygen glows a soft green roughly 100 kilometres up and a rare, deep red higher still; nitrogen rims the lower edges in blue and violet. Because the particles are funnelled by Earth's magnetic field, the display crowds into an oval ring centred on the geomagnetic pole, brightest when the Sun is restless.

Why it forms a ring

Earth behaves like a vast bar magnet. The solar wind cannot easily cross its field lines, so incoming particles are steered toward the two magnetic poles, where the field dips down into the atmosphere. The result is the auroral oval — a halo some 3,000 kilometres across that thickens and drifts equatorward during magnetic storms, which is why the lights are occasionally seen far to the south.

A history of watching

Long before the physics was known, the lights were read as omens and stories. Norse sailors called them the glint of the Valkyries' armour; Sámi herders hushed their children beneath them. The Italian name aurora borrows the Roman goddess of dawn, and borealis the north wind Boreas — a label fixed by Galileo's circle in the 1600s. Only in the twentieth century did instruments on the ground and, later, satellites overhead tie the glow firmly to the Sun.
